Executive Summary

Introduction

Improving workplace safety is not only a beneficial policy for employees health but the business as a whole. Fewer Injuries means paying out less for workers compensation and employees spend more days working at full health. While there is a general perception that blue collar work such as manufacturing and construction workers are at a higher risk of injury due to the physical nature of such work, recent studies have questioned that line of logic. White caller workers are exposed to many of the same risks that blue collar workers face such as falls, slips, repetitive motion and bodily injuries which make up over half of all reported injuries in the United States [citation one].

This report evaluates whether office workers are at a greater risk of injury compared to non-office workers across thousands of companies. Using data from Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) the analysis will consider both the probability of injury occurrence and the severity of injuries. The goal of this report is to determine whether injuries that are sustained by office workers or non-office workers have a more detrimental effect on company productivity.

Methodology

This study will examine differences in workplace injury rates and severity between office and non-office workers to determine which type of injury has a more detrimental effect on the company. We will use data from the Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) Injury Tracking Application 300A dataset for the calendar year 2025. The OSHA Injury Tracking Application 300A dataset is a record of all employers reported workplace injuries and contains total hours worked at the company, number of recorded incidents at the company, days away from work due to injury, day restricted at work due to injury, and North American Industry Classification System (NAICS) codes. The dataset is publicly available on the OSHA website.

Classification of Workers

To compare injury rates we categorized instances into two groups office and non-office. Classification was based on industry codes (NAICS). Office based codes were “Finance and Insurance”, “Information”, “Professional, Scientific, and Technical Services”, “Management of Companies and Enterprises”, where work is primarily defined by sedentary work and performed in office environments. Non-office based codes were “Manufacturing”, “Construction”, “Utilities”, “Transportation and Warehousing”, “Agriculture, Forestry, Fishing and Hunting”, “Mining, Quarrying, and Oil and Gas Extraction”, where workers are more likely to do physical labor or face environmental hazards.A notable exception from the office group is public administration this was left out as it contained both clerical works and police and firefighters making it had to be placed in either office or non-office groups. Similar to public administration any NAICS code not listed before was left out of the analysis because the Industry contained a mix of office and non-office jobs making it difficult to place in a single group.

Variables and Measures

To account for the variability of type of work that was done whether it was full time, part time or over time the analysis will examine metrics on a per hour basis rather than the traditional per employee bases that is commonly used. We also make the assumption that an injury that had more days that the individual was away from work or restricted on the job was a more severe injury.

Conclusion

This report examined injuries among office and non-office workers and found that while office workers experience injuries at a higher overall frequency, non-office workers suffer injuries that are more severe on a per injury basis and lead to more days away or restricted at work. It is when we account for both the injury frequency and the injury severity that we can see that overall loss of productivity is the same. By comparing the number of days that are lost per 100,000 hours worked we find that the office workers and non office workers lose the same amount of days not able to work. Any company would want to reduce the number of days their employees could not work but the strategies employed could be representative to the type of work and value done by office and non-office employees. These differences may reflect how companies perceive the replaceability of employees. Office workers often possess specialized knowledge or Institutional knowledge that are difficult to replace quickly. As a result a greater emphasis is placed on preventing long term absences and ensuring that if there is an injury it is addressed before becoming severe. In contrast non-office workers participate in manual labor positions where replacement labor may be easier or less difficult to train. Meaning companies may be less incentivised to properly protect non-office workers, which could contribute to more severe injuries observed in these occupations.

Several limitations should be noted when interpreting the findings of this report. First, companies with fewer than 250 employees are not always required by law to report injuries unless they have received a written request from OSHA or the BLS which means that there are some workplace injuries that were not included in the dataset. Secondly the Injury Tracking Application does not track when an injury has been sustained on the job making it impossible for the employee to continue potentially underrepresenting the severity of injuries. Third the NAICS codes classify the work of the company rather than the specific responsibilities of individual employees at the company limiting the reliability of comparing across industry types. Finally, workplace injuries may be underreported due to company culture or lack of awareness regarding regulatory requirements.

As for how to reduce general workplace injuries across both office and non-office environments companies should follow common sense safety measures. Maintaining clean and well organized workspaces can reduce slips, trips, and the spread of illness. Regular training of employees about relevant safety procedures and regulatory requirements to ensure workers remain informed. Providing appropriate and effective personal protective equipment (PPE) for what employees need on the job will minimize exposure to workplace hazards.Additionally, ensuring tools, machinery, and vehicles have maintenance done regularly can help prevent unforeseen malfunctions that lead to accidents. The implementation or continuation of these general guidelines can improve employee safety and improve company performance by reducing the number of days an employee is not able to perform their responsibilities.
